About the program

Training the virologists public health needs.

COVID-19, AIDS, smallpox, polio, influenza, viral gastroenteritis and hepatitis illustrate why viral pathogens must be studied from a global health perspective. Public health depends on well-trained, imaginative virologists.

Philosophy

Why virology, and why now

The world population is projected to keep growing in the coming decades, mostly in developing countries. Global trade, shifting insect vector populations, climate change, pressure on agricultural resources and the possibility of bioterrorism all heighten the threat posed by viral disease.

Despite advances in vaccines and antiviral drugs, viral diseases remain a major cause of illness and death worldwide. The 2001 release of Bacillus anthracis in the United States made the threat of bioterrorism concrete and underscored the need for effective medical countermeasures.

Viruses are also valuable research tools for studying cellular processes, and they hold promise as expression and delivery systems for vaccines and therapeutics.

Program focus

Two training tracks

The program focuses its training on two key areas of modern virology.

01

Host-virus interactions

Virus-cell interactions, antiviral innate immunity and immune evasion. Viruses are obligate parasites with limited coding capacity, so they hijack host factors and machinery to replicate. Studying these interactions reveals how cells handle gene expression and regulation, RNA biology, communication between cells, and protein and membrane trafficking.

Cells mount robust defenses against viruses, and studying these defenses yields insight into both virus and host. Viruses in turn evade innate and adaptive immunity, which allows them to replicate, spread and cause disease. Understanding these evasion mechanisms informs efforts to prevent and treat infection.

  • Virus-cell biology
  • Innate immunity
  • Immune evasion
02

Viral immunology & vaccine design

Many vaccines use attenuated (“live”) viruses, and attenuating mutations often sit in replicase proteins or non-coding regions of the genome, so understanding replication explains attenuation. Knowledge of adaptive immune responses guides vaccine design, and the most successful vaccines induce both mucosal and systemic immunity.

Program faculty engineer viral antigens that elicit broadly neutralizing antibody and T cell responses against HIV, influenza and dengue. Because most viruses enter through the mucosal surfaces of the respiratory, gastrointestinal and urogenital tracts, program faculty are also developing mucosal vaccine delivery platforms for HIV, influenza, RSV, SARS-CoV-2 and herpesviruses.

Training

Human, animal and plant virologists in one program

The program brings together human, animal and plant virologists. This integrative approach broadens each trainee's research perspective and skills, and gives trainees access to an exceptionally broad range of institutions and mentor expertise.

Trainees gain first-hand experience in laboratories at both academic and government institutions, an advantage in a competitive job market. Graduate training in virology also prepares trainees for work in related disciplines and for careers as independent scientists.

  • Molecular biology
  • Structural biology
  • Biochemistry
  • Cell biology
  • Epidemiology
  • Genomics
  • Vaccine research

The program offers these opportunities to highly motivated students, with special outreach to students from minority groups, to strengthen its contribution to global public health.
