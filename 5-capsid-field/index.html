Graduate training in virology across Maryland.

One graduate program spanning four institutions. Trainees work with 22 faculty mentors at the University of Maryland, the National Institutes of Health, the Virginia-Maryland College of Veterinary Medicine and the Institute for Bioscience and Biotechnology Research, whose research addresses HIVHIV, influenza, herpesviruses, coronaviruses, vaccine design, viral evolution, structural virology and plant viruses.

The program

Two research tracks, one training network.

The program brings together laboratories that study plant, animal and human viruses, giving trainees access to mentors and expertise across four institutions.

Track 01

Host-virus interactions

How viruses exploit host cells, how cells respond through innate immunity, and how viruses evade those defenses, in human, animal and plant systems.

  • Virus-cell biology
  • Innate immunity
  • Immune evasion
Read more → Track 02

Viral immunology & vaccine design

Engineered viral antigens that elicit broad antibody and T cell responses, and mucosal vaccine delivery platforms.

  • HIV
  • Influenza
  • Dengue
  • RSV
  • SARS-CoV-2
  • Herpesviruses
Read more → Mentoring

 Monthly meetings

Group meetings

Meet a mentor

Faculty spotlight

AS
UMD College ParkAnne E. SimonAnne Simon studies RNA viruses of plants, especially umbravirus-like viruses that lack conventional movement proteins. She spent 13 years on the faculty of the University of Massachusetts before joining Maryland in 2000, and co-founded Silvec Biologics, which develops RNA viruses as vectors for plant therapeutics.
EF
NIHEric O. FreedEric Freed has led NCI's HIV Dynamics and Replication Program since 2015. He is founding editor-in-chief of Viruses, an editor of the Journal of Molecular Biology, and received the KT Jeang Retrovirology Prize in 2018.
TI
NIHTijana IvanovicTijana Ivanovic developed TIRF-based single-particle methods for studying viral membrane fusion as a postdoc with Steve Harrison at Harvard, then ran her own lab at Brandeis University before moving to NIH in 2023.
YL
IBBRYuxing LiYuxing Li's earlier work on broadly neutralizing antibody responses to HIV-1 helped lead to antibodies that target the envelope's receptor binding site.
